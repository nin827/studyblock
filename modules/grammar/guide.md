# Grammar: how to solve

## General
- Read the whole sentence first; the job a word does depends on its context.
- Try each option in the sentence and say it in your head. Then check it against the rule, because "sounds right" can mislead.
- The examples follow standard US English. The word to identify is shown in **bold**.

## Parts of speech
1. Ask what the bold word is doing in *this* sentence, and match the job (see the list below).
2. The same word can change role: "a **fast** car" (adjective) vs "drives **fast**" (adverb).
3. A word like *before* is a preposition before a noun ("before lunch") but a conjunction before a whole clause ("before we ate").

- Names a person, place, thing or idea → **noun**
- Stands in for a noun (*they*, *she*, *it*) → **pronoun**
- An action or state → **verb**
- Describes a noun → **adjective**
- Describes a verb, adjective or adverb (how, when, where) → **adverb**
- Shows position or relation before a noun (*under*, *on*) → **preposition**
- Joins words or clauses (*and*, *but*, *because*) → **conjunction**
- A burst of feeling (*Oh!*) → **interjection**

Watch out: not every adverb ends in *-ly* (*outside*, *often*, *very*).

## Verb tenses
1. Find the helper verbs, then name the tense from the pattern (see the table below).
2. Use the **past perfect** (*had* + participle) for the earlier of two past events.
3. Irregular verbs: learn the three forms together, like *sing–sang–sung*, *swim–swam–swum*, *write–wrote–written*.
4. In formal "if" wishes and imagined situations, use **were** for every subject.

| Pattern | Tense |
| --- | --- |
| walks / walk | simple present |
| was/were + -ing | past continuous |
| has/have + past participle | present perfect |
| had + past participle | past perfect |
| will have + past participle | future perfect |
| has/have been + -ing | present perfect continuous |

Watch out: the past participle (*swum*) needs a helper (*have swum*); the simple past (*swam*) stands alone.

## Punctuation
1. **Apostrophes**: one owner → `'s` (the dog's bowl); plural owners ending in s → `s'` (the dogs' bowls). Contractions mark missing letters (*don't*, *it's* = it is).
2. **Comma + joining word** (and, but, so) links two full sentences. A comma alone cannot.
3. **Semicolon** joins two closely linked full sentences without a joining word.
4. **Colon** introduces a list or explanation after a complete sentence.
5. Commas around a *who/which* clause mean it is extra information; leave them out if it identifies which one.
6. Hyphenate two words acting as one adjective *before* a noun (*a well-known author*).

Watch out: *its* (belonging to it) has no apostrophe.

## Agreement
1. Find the real subject, and ignore phrases in between (*of the boxes*, *along with her teammates*).
2. Singular subject → singular verb (*is*, *has*, *barks*); plural → plural verb (*are*, *have*, *bark*).
3. *Each*, *every*, *one of*, *either*, *neither* used alone are singular.
4. With *either … or* / *neither … nor*, the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject.
5. Subjects that end in *-s* but name one thing (*mathematics*, *news*) are singular.
6. Pronouns match their noun: one thing → *its*; several people → *their*.

Watch out: "The box of chocolates" is about one box, so it takes *is*.
