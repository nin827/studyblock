# Chords: how to solve

## General

- Chords are built in thirds: take every other letter name from the root (C, E, G, B).
- Get the letters right first, then pick sharps or flats to get the right interval sizes.
- A major 3rd is 4 semitones, a minor 3rd 3. A perfect 5th is 7, diminished 6, augmented 8.
- Every note in a chord has a different letter, so D, G♭, A can't be a triad.

## Triads

1. Write three letters from the root, skipping one each time: D, F, A.
2. Measure the 3rd from the root: major 3rd (4 semitones) or minor 3rd (3).
3. Measure the 5th from the root: perfect (7), diminished (6) or augmented (8).
4. Adjust the accidentals on the 3rd and 5th until the sizes match the chord type.

Example: D major needs a major 3rd above D. D to F is only 3 semitones, so raise it: **D, F♯, A**.

**Watch out:** a diminished triad has a minor 3rd **and** a diminished 5th: B, D, F.

**Blocks:** "Which notes make…" questions are answered with note blocks. Place them from the root up (root, 3rd, 5th, then 7th for seventh chords). Some blocks are wrong spellings, such as G♭ instead of F♯ or a 3rd with the wrong accidental: leave those in the bank. Keep the letter even when that needs a double sharp or flat: on Hard, E♭ diminished is E♭, G♭, B𝄫 (B𝄫 sounds like A, but the 5th must be a B).

**Expert: working backwards and altering.** If a note is the 3rd of a triad, the root is 3 letters below it; if it is the 5th, 5 letters below. Then choose the accidental that gives the right size (G♯ as the 3rd of a major triad: E). When one note moves a semitone with the same letter, the letters still stack in thirds, so remeasure from the bottom letter: raising the root of C major gives C♯, E, G, which is C♯ diminished.

## Identify

1. Rearrange the notes so they stack in thirds (every other letter).
2. The bottom note of that stack is the root.
3. Measure the 3rd and 5th from the root to get the quality.
4. Look at the bass (the lowest note as written): root = root position, 3rd = 1st inversion, 5th = 2nd inversion.

Example: E, G, C restacks as C, E, G. The root is C, it is major, and E (the 3rd) is in the bass, so it is **C major, 1st inversion**.

**Watch out:** the lowest note is not always the root. Only in root position is the bass the root.

**Expert: sevenths and working backwards.** Seventh chords have a 3rd inversion too: the 7th in the bass. B, D, F, G restacks as G, B, D, F, so it is G7, 1st inversion. Given only the bass of an inversion, go down from it: in 1st inversion the bass is the 3rd, in 2nd the 5th, in 3rd the 7th. Augmented triads sound the same in every inversion, so only the spelling shows the root: E, G♯, C is C augmented, 1st inversion.

## Chords in a key

1. Write the major scale with its key signature.
2. Find the degree you need: I is the 1st note, IV the 4th, and so on.
3. Stack every other note of the scale on it: three notes.
4. The quality follows the pattern: I, IV, V major; ii, iii, vi minor; vii° diminished.

Example: in G major (G A B C D E F♯), vi is E, G, B: **E minor**.

**Watch out:** keep the key signature: in D major the iii chord is F♯ minor, not F minor.

**Expert: keys and minor keys.** To find the key of a chord with a given numeral, the chord's root is that degree of the scale: go down the letters to the tonic and check the interval (ii is a major 2nd above the tonic, vi a major 6th). An inverted chord's numeral comes from its root, not its bass. In a minor key, use the harmonic minor: i, ii°, III+, iv, V, VI, vii°. If the 7th is already sharp in the key signature, raising it makes a double sharp: G♯ minor's leading note is F𝄪, so its V chord is D♯, F𝄪, A♯.

## Seventh chords

1. Stack four letters in thirds from the root: G, B, D, F.
2. Build the triad first: major for 7 and maj7, minor for m7.
3. Add the 7th: a minor 7th (10 semitones) for 7 and m7, a major 7th (11) for maj7.
4. For V7 in a key, start on the 5th note of the scale and take scale notes 5, 7, 2 and 4.

Example: G7 is G, B, D plus a minor 7th, F: **G, B, D, F**. It is V7 in C major.

**Watch out:** the 7th of a dominant 7th is a whole tone below the root (G7 has F, not F♯); maj7 is only a semitone below.

**Expert: diatonic sevenths and V7 backwards.** Stacking four scale notes on each degree of a major key gives Imaj7, ii7, iii7, IVmaj7, V7, vi7, viiø7 (m7 on ii, iii and vi; on vii a diminished triad plus a minor 7th, the half-diminished m7♭5: in C major, B, D, F, A is Bm7♭5). If a chord is V7, its key's tonic is a perfect 5th below the root (D7 is V7 in G major and in G minor); the chord's 3rd is the leading note.
