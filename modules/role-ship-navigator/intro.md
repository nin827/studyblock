> [!WARNING] For learning only
> These are arithmetic scenarios with made-up ships, places and figures. Every rule (the rule of twelfths, the cube rule for fuel, safety limits, reserves) is set for the exercise, not taken from a tide table, a chart or the rules of the road. It is not a way to plan a real passage.

# Applied scenarios (Ship Navigator)

Scenario questions for the **Ship Navigator** role, from Medium to Expert+. Each one is a single made-up scenario with several linked answers, and each answer uses a different skill: rates and clock arithmetic, vectors, trigonometry, fractions of a range, and a yes or no decision. A later part usually builds on an earlier one, so work in order. The calculator is on, and the numbers from the prompt are already in it where that helps.

Speeds are in knots (kt), distances in nautical miles (nm, 1 nm = 1852 m), bearings are true, in degrees clockwise from north and written with three figures. Times use the 24-hour clock with fixed UTC offsets and no daylight saving. Every part says how to round.

## Passage time, ETA and fuel

A distance and a speed give the time at sea, then the fuel burned. Harder questions add the arrival time in another time zone, a fuel reserve and a check against the fuel on board. At Expert+ the ship slows down to save fuel under a stated cube rule, and you weigh the saving against the berth window.

## Set, drift and course made good

A current has a set (the direction it flows toward) and a drift (its speed). Along the heading it simply adds or subtracts; at right angles or at other angles you split it into along and across parts to find the ground speed, the course made good and how far the ship is carried off line. At Expert+ you work backwards to the course to steer for a wanted track.

## Tides and under-keel clearance

The rule of twelfths gives the height of tide hour by hour between low and high water. Combined with a charted depth and a draught it gives the under-keel clearance and whether it is safe. At Expert+ you find the earliest time a ship can cross a shallow bar.

## Closest approach of two ships

Two ships hold course and speed. Head-on or overtaking, the gap closes at the sum or difference of the speeds. For crossing ships you work with the relative velocity (the other ship's velocity minus your own) to find the time and distance of closest approach. At Expert+ you start from two radar plots of the other ship.
