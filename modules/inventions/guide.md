# Inventions: how to solve

## General
- Hang each invention on a timeline and a place: which century, which country, which problem it solved.
- Names often hide in words we still use: Morse code, the diesel engine, the watt, the volt, pasteurisation, the Nobel Prize.
- For years, type the year only. Check whether the question wants a full name, a surname only, or one word.
- Date questions say how much to give: the year, the month and year, or the full date. `July 1969`, `Jul 1969`, `20 July 1969`, `July 20, 1969` and `20/7/1969` all work; numeric dates are read day first, so check the "Read as" line. One unit off (a year, a month or a day, at the level asked) earns half credit, so a close guess is worth giving.

## Communication
1. Put the steps in order: papyrus (ancient Egypt) → paper (China) → movable-type printing (Gutenberg, 1400s) → telegraph (Morse, 1840s) → telephone (Bell, 1876) → World Wide Web (Berners-Lee, 1989).
2. Link each person to a place: Gutenberg ↔ Mainz, Berners-Lee ↔ CERN in Switzerland, ARPANET ↔ US defence funding.
3. To place a century, add one to the leading digits: the 1400s are the **15th** century.

Watch out: the Internet (the network) and the World Wide Web (pages on it) are different inventions.

## Transport
1. Order by power source: balloon (1780s) → steam (Watt's engine, then railway locomotives in the 1800s) → petrol car (Benz, 1880s) → powered aeroplane (Wright brothers, 1903) → helicopter (Sikorsky).
2. Separate *invented* from *mass-produced*: Benz built an early car; Ford made cars cheap with the moving assembly line (Model T, 1908).
3. Steam pioneers: Watt improved the engine; Trevithick built an early locomotive; the Stephensons' *Rocket* won the Rainhill Trials.
4. Pairs of brothers: Montgolfier (balloon, France) and Wright (aeroplane, USA).

Watch out: James Watt did not invent the steam engine; he made it much more efficient.

## Medicine
1. Match each breakthrough to the problem it solved (see the list below).
2. Germ theory pioneers: Pasteur (heating food to kill microbes) and Koch (the bacteria behind specific diseases).
3. The Curies worked on radioactivity and found new radioactive elements in the 1890s.

- Preventing disease → vaccines (Jenner in the 1790s; the polio vaccine in the 1950s)
- Killing bacteria → antibiotics (penicillin, found by accident on a mouldy dish)
- Seeing inside the body → X-rays (1890s, Germany)
- Listening to the chest → stethoscope (1810s, France)

Watch out: a vaccine *prevents* infection; an antibiotic *treats* a bacterial one.

## Everyday technology
1. Units named after people are written in lower case: watt (Watt), volt (Volta).
2. Edison's lab at Menlo Park produced the phonograph and a practical light bulb.
3. Many inventions were accidents: microwave cooking came from radar work; Velcro from burrs on a dog's coat.
4. Electronics: Volta's pile was the first battery (1800); the transistor came from Bell Labs (1947).
5. Others to anchor: Baird demonstrated television (1920s); Baekeland made Bakelite plastic; Nobel invented dynamite.

Watch out: Edison improved and sold the light bulb rather than inventing the very first one; the phonograph was his own original invention.
