# How to solve: Applied scenarios (Music Theorist)

## General

- Read the whole scenario first, then work the parts in order: later parts usually reuse earlier results.
- Write the major scale of the key out (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half) before anything else. Chords, signatures and Roman numerals all come from it.
- Spelled answers use each letter name once in a scale; check your accidentals against the key signature.
- A quarter note is one beat, and the tempo counts quarter notes per minute. One beat lasts 60 ÷ tempo seconds.
- Keep full precision in the calculator and round only when you type each answer. Using an earlier rounded answer still gives the same later answer.

## Key and chords

1. Write the major scale and number its notes 1 to 7.
2. The triad on a degree takes every other scale note from that degree: root, third and fifth.
3. Its quality follows from the notes: major (major third, perfect fifth), minor (minor third, perfect fifth), diminished (minor third, diminished fifth).
4. A chord belongs to the key when every one of its notes is in the scale.
5. Working backwards: subtract the degree's interval from the chord root to reach the tonic.

## Progressions and cadences

1. A Roman numeral is a scale degree: upper case means a major triad, lower case a minor triad.
2. Turn a chord name into a numeral by finding its root in the scale.
3. A cadence depends only on the last two chords, and the rule is given in the question.
4. Given one chord and its numeral, go back to the tonic, then rebuild every other chord.

## Tempo and bars

1. Bar length in seconds = beats per bar × 60 ÷ tempo.
2. Length of a section = bars × bar length. Add sections for the whole piece.
3. Working backwards: tempo = total beats × 60 ÷ seconds.
4. Compare your total with the stated slot for a yes or no answer, and compare tempo with the ranges for a marking.

## Transposing parts

1. Moving up an interval: count the letter names (a 3rd up from E is G), then fix the accidental so the size is right.
2. Moving down works the same way, counting letters downwards.
3. A new key's signature comes from writing its major scale and counting sharps or flats.
4. Instruments that sound lower than written: the concert key is the written key moved down by the stated interval.
5. Two keys: count letters from tonic to tonic for the number, and semitones for the quality.
