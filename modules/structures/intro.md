# Structures

The first ideas of structural engineering: how forces turn things, how a material stretches under load, what the supports of a beam carry, and how much margin a design keeps.

| Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| moment = force × perpendicular distance | N·m; balance means clockwise = anticlockwise |
| stress σ = F ÷ A | 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa; 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa; 1 GPa = 1000 MPa |
| strain ε = ΔL ÷ L | no units; × 100 for a percentage |
| Young's modulus E = σ ÷ ε | so ΔL = F × L ÷ (A × E) |
| beam in equilibrium | upward forces = downward forces, and moments about any point cancel |
| factor of safety = failure stress ÷ working stress | the same ratio works with loads |

**Conventions:** forces are in N or kN, lengths in m or mm, and every question states which. 1 m² = 10⁶ mm². Beams are horizontal and loads act vertically downward. A *light* beam has no weight; a *uniform* beam's weight acts at its midpoint. A UDL (uniformly distributed load) of w kN/m over a length is treated as one force w × length acting at the middle of that length. Shear force at a section is the sum of the vertical forces to its left, upward positive. Where g is needed it is stated (10 or 9.81 N/kg). The stress or load at failure is a given figure, and every standard value a question uses is stated in it. This is general textbook practice, not a design code.

> [!TIP]
> Write the units next to every number as you go. Most mistakes here are a mix of mm and m, or N and kN.

| Level | What to expect |
| --- | --- |
| Easy | One formula: a moment, a stress, a reaction from one load, a factor of safety |
| Medium | Unit conversions, see-saws and levers, two loads, safe loads and cable counts |
| Hard | Pivots with a heavy beam, Young's modulus and extension, three loads or an overhang, checking a section |
| Expert | Two unknown reactions or a tipping plank, sizing a rod for stress and extension, a UDL with the shear at a point, choosing a standard section |
