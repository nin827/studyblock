# Fluids & Hydraulics

How engineers move liquids around: how much flows through a pipe, how a small push on oil lifts a car, how much power a pump needs and what it costs to run, and how fast a tank fills or empties.

| Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Q = A × v | flow rate (m³/s) = cross-sectional area (m²) × average speed (m/s) |
| A1 × v1 = A2 × v2 | continuity: the same flow passes every section of a full pipe |
| area of a circle = π × d² ÷ 4 | so halving a pipe's diameter makes the water 4 times faster |
| F1 ÷ A1 = F2 ÷ A2 | Pascal's principle: the same pressure acts on every piston |
| F = p × A | piston force; 1 bar = 100 000 Pa = 0.1 N/mm² |
| P = ρ × g × Q × H, or P = p × Q | hydraulic power in W |
| efficiency = output ÷ input | input power = output ÷ efficiency |
| time = volume ÷ net flow | net flow = flow in − flow out |

**Conventions:** water has a density of 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 N/kg, and every question that uses them says so. 1 m³ = 1000 L, so 1 L of water has a mass of 1 kg. Pipes run full, and the speed is the average across the pipe. Oil and water cannot be squashed, and friction is ignored unless an efficiency is given. Energy costs use a generic `$`.

> [!TIP]
> Convert first: mm to m (÷ 1000), L/s to m³/s (÷ 1000), L/min to L/s (÷ 60), bar to N/mm² (× 0.1). Most slips here are a unit left behind.

| Level | What to expect |
| --- | --- |
| Easy | One formula: Q = A × v, a unit change, a mechanical advantage, a box tank's volume |
| Medium | Round pipes and bores, flow in L/s, cylinder force from pressure, efficiency, net filling rates |
| Hard | Pipes that narrow or branch, push and pull of a double-acting cylinder, a lever-worked jack, power to running cost, level rise rates |
| Expert | Choosing a pipe or bore from a catalogue, nozzles, cylinder speeds and cycle times, comparing pumps with payback, working back to a pump flow or supply rate |
