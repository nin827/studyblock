# Intervals & Scales: how to solve

## General

- Picture a piano keyboard. One key to the next, black or white, is one semitone.
- E–F and B–C have no black key between them, so they are only a semitone apart.
- Letters first, accidentals second: get the letter name right by counting letters, then pick the sharp, flat or natural that gives the right size.
- Each scale uses every letter name exactly once.

## Notes

1. For semitone counts, start on the first note and move up one key at a time.
2. Count the white-key letters first (a tone between most neighbours, a semitone for E–F and B–C).
3. Adjust for sharps and flats: a sharp is one key higher than its letter, a flat one key lower.
4. For an enharmonic name, find the key, then name it from the neighbouring letter.

Example: from D up to F is D–E (2) plus E–F (1), so **3** semitones.

**Watch out:** E♯ is the same key as F, and C♭ is the same key as B.

**Expert: backwards and fixed letters.** "Y is n semitones above which note?" means count n keys *down* from Y (or 12 − n up). To spell a key with a given letter, find the key first, then decide whether it is that letter, its sharp or its flat: one semitone above E is F, but written with E it is E♯.

## Intervals

1. Count the letter names from the lower note to the upper one, counting both. That gives the number (3rd, 5th…).
2. Count the semitones between the two notes.
3. Compare with the major or perfect interval of that number: 2nd 2, 3rd 4, 4th 5, 5th 7, 6th 9, 7th 11, octave 12.
4. One semitone smaller than major is minor. One larger than major or perfect is augmented. One smaller than minor or perfect is diminished.

Example: D up to F♯ spans D, E, F (a 3rd) and 4 semitones, so it is a **major 3rd**.

**Watch out:** F♯ and G♭ sound the same, but D to G♭ spans four letters, so it is a diminished 4th, not a major 3rd.

**Expert: below, inversions and compounds.** For a note *below*, count the letters down first, then fix the accidental by the size. Inverting an interval: the numbers add to 9, major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, perfect stays perfect, and the semitones add to 12. A compound interval (9th to 13th) is the simple one plus an octave: subtract 7 from the number, add 12 to the semitones. A major 10th above C is E, 16 semitones.

## Major & minor scales

1. Write the letter names from the tonic, each one once.
2. Add the key signature's sharps or flats.
3. For harmonic minor, raise the 7th note by a semitone.
4. Check against the step pattern.

Example: G major is G, A, B, C, D, E, F♯. Its 7th note is **F♯**.

**Watch out:** a minor scale uses the key signature of its relative major (a minor 3rd higher): E minor has one sharp, like G major.

**Expert: working backwards and the raised 7th.** To find which scale a note belongs to, count the letters down to the tonic (the note is degree n, so go down n − 1 letters), then pick the accidental that gives the right interval from the tonic. The parallel minor (same tonic) lowers the 3rd, 6th and 7th of the major. In harmonic minor the raised 7th makes an augmented 2nd from the 6th, and diminished intervals from the 7th up to the 3rd, 4th and 6th above it.

| Scale | Step pattern (T = tone, S = semitone) |
| --- | --- |
| Major | T T S T T T S |
| Natural minor | T S T T S T T |
| Harmonic minor | T S T T S T+S S |

## Key signatures

1. Sharp keys go up the circle of fifths from C: G (1 sharp), D (2), A (3), E (4), B (5), F♯ (6), C♯ (7).
2. Flat keys go the other way: F (1 flat), B♭ (2), E♭ (3), A♭ (4), D♭ (5), G♭ (6), C♭ (7).
3. Sharps are added in the order F C G D A E B; flats in the order B E A D G C F.
4. The relative minor is a minor 3rd below the major key and shares its signature.

Example: three flats are B♭, E♭, A♭, which is **E♭ major** or its relative, **C minor**.

**Watch out:** the parallel minor (same letter) is not the relative minor: C major and C minor have different key signatures.

**Expert: enharmonic keys and accidentals.** At the bottom of the circle, keys overlap: B = C♭, F♯ = G♭, C♯ = D♭ (and their relative minors). Their sharps and flats add up to 12. The parallel minor is 3 steps flatwards from its major. A signature fits a major key and its relative minor; a recurring accidental a semitone below the minor tonic (its raised 7th) points to the minor key.
