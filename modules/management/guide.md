# Management: how to solve

## General

- Find the framework named in the question, then sort the facts it gives into that framework's boxes.
- With block answers, settle one column at a time: each column is a separate decision.

## Motivation (Maslow, Herzberg)

1. **Maslow**, bottom to top: physiological (food, rest, basic pay), safety (security, a safe workplace, pensions), social (belonging, colleagues), esteem (recognition, status, promotion), self-actualisation (challenge, reaching your potential).
2. People are motivated by the lowest unmet level and move up one level at a time.
3. **Herzberg's hygiene factors** surround the job: pay, conditions, job security, supervision, company policy, relationships and status. Improving them **removes dissatisfaction** but does not motivate.
4. **Motivators** come from the work itself: achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement and growth. Improving them **increases motivation**.

**Watch out:** a grander job title or bigger office meets *esteem* needs for Maslow, but Herzberg lists status as a *hygiene factor*.

## Leadership and McGregor

Ask who makes the decision:

- the manager alone, giving orders: **autocratic**
- the manager after taking the team's input: **democratic**
- the team itself, with little direction: **laissez-faire**
- the manager alone, but putting staff welfare first and explaining: **paternalistic**

1. **McGregor's Theory X:** workers dislike work, avoid responsibility and need control. **Theory Y:** workers enjoy meaningful work and seek responsibility.
2. Theory X managers tend to be autocratic; Theory Y managers tend to consult (democratic) or delegate (laissez-faire).

| Style | Communication |
| --- | --- |
| Autocratic | One-way, top down |
| Democratic | Two-way, with discussion |
| Laissez-faire | Little direction given |
| Paternalistic | Top down, with explanation |

## Organisational structure

1. **Span of control** = the number of people reporting *directly* to one manager.
2. **Levels of hierarchy** count every layer, including the top person and the bottom layer.
3. With the same span s everywhere, each level has s times as many people as the one above: 1, s, s², …
4. **Chain of command:** to count the people between two roles, count only the roles strictly between them.
5. **Tall** structures have many levels and narrow spans, so messages pass through more people (slower). **Flat** structures have few levels and wide spans (faster).
6. **Delayering** removes a level: the jobs cut are everyone on that level, and the managers above take on their reports, so spans widen.

Example: 1 chief executive, span 4, 3 levels: 1 + 4 + 16 = 21 people.

**Expert.** *Planning with a maximum span:* divide the front-line staff by the maximum span and **round up**, then repeat until you reach one person. Each result is a level of managers. *Reorganising:* staff = managers × span; the new span = staff ÷ new number of managers.

## Porter's generic strategies

1. **Scope:** broad (the whole market) or narrow (a niche).
2. **Advantage:** lower cost or uniqueness (differentiation).
3. With data, use the thresholds the question states: unit cost at least 10% below the industry average is a cost advantage; a price at least 15% above the average is uniqueness. % difference = (firm − average) ÷ average × 100.

| | Lower cost | Uniqueness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broad | Cost leadership | Differentiation |
| Narrow | Cost focus | Differentiation focus |

**Expert.** A broad firm with neither advantage is **stuck in the middle**.
